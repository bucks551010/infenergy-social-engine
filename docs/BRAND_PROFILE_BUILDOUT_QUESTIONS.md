# Brand Profile Buildout Questions

Answer in plain language. Specific examples beat polished marketing language. Each answer will
be retained as owner-controlled brand guidance, not treated as a claim about a product unless it
has separate evidence.

## 1. The Real Origin

1. What actually happened during the outage or storm that made this business necessary to you?
2. What did you see, hear, smell, or have to do that a product catalog would never capture?
3. What did you wish someone had told you before that moment?
4. What part of the current origin story feels too polished, incomplete, or unlike your voice?

## 2. The Customer You Refuse To Ignore

1. Whose problem do you understand unusually well? Describe one real person or situation.
2. What are they worried about saying out loud? What are they worried about privately?
3. What mistakes do they make before buying power equipment?
4. What would make them feel respected instead of sold to?
5. Which audience segments are most important in the next 12 months? Rank them and say why.

## 3. The Promise And Its Limits

1. Complete this sentence in your own words: "Infenergy exists so that people can..."
2. What can customers reasonably expect from you beyond the device itself?
3. What should a customer never assume your products, guidance, or service can do?
4. What situations, loads, products, or outcomes should we plainly say are not a fit?
5. What claim would you rather lose a sale than make?

## 4. The Point Of View

1. What do competitors or the category get wrong about preparedness?
2. What do you believe about fear-based emergency marketing?
3. Where do you disagree with "bigger is always better" or "buy the highest capacity" advice?
4. What does responsible preparedness look like to you?
5. What should people feel after encountering the brand, even if they never buy?

## 5. Voice And Language

1. Give three phrases you naturally say to customers that sound unmistakably like you.
2. Give five words or phrases that should never appear in brand copy.
3. When should the brand sound urgent, and when should it slow down and reassure?
4. How direct should a call to action be on Facebook, Instagram, and LinkedIn?
5. What kinds of humor, if any, fit? What would immediately feel disrespectful or fake?

## 6. Proof And Trust

1. What skeptical question do customers ask before trusting you?
2. What proof can you genuinely provide: fit process, catalog facts, policy, service behavior,
   customer permissioned stories, or something else?
3. When have you advised someone not to buy a particular item? What made that the right call?
4. Which details make you trust a business selling preparedness equipment?
5. What would make a post feel manipulative, even if technically accurate?

## 7. Visual Identity

1. What real Gulf Coast places, rooms, vehicles, times of day, weather, or materials belong in
   the brand's visual world?
2. What places or visual styles should never appear?
3. Which existing product photographs are accurate enough to use as approved references?
4. When a person appears, what should their expression, activity, and situation feel like?
5. What image would falsely imply a product capability, preparedness outcome, or lifestyle?

## 8. Content Territory

1. Which real tensions should the brand be allowed to name? Write them as felt contradictions,
   not slogans.
2. Which topics should be education-only, with no product mentioned?
3. Which hard questions should the brand answer publicly?
4. Which seasonal or local moments matter before, during, and after hurricane season?
5. What would make someone glad they saw a post even if they never purchase?

## How These Answers Are Used

- Origin, customer, point-of-view, and tension answers populate the owner-approved Human Truth
  repository.
- Limits, proof, and prohibited wording strengthen claim, trust, and prompt-governance gates.
- Visual answers define prompt constraints before image rendering.
- Content-territory answers drive tension-first selection and education/product/brand balance.

Do not include finished captions or hooks. Raw examples, stories, and exact customer wording are
more useful than polished copy.
